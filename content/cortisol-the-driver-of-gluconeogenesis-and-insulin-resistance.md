### Cortisol: The Driver of Gluconeogenesis and Insulin Resistance

Cortisol's reputation as the "stress hormone" often obscures its precise and devastating role as a metabolic saboteur. Its primary mission during a crisis is to ensure the brain has a steady supply of glucose, and it will achieve this by any means necessary—even if it means dismantling your own body to do so.




This process is called **gluconeogenesis**—literally, "the creation of new glucose." Cortisol is the primary driver of this catabolic process. It sends a powerful signal to break down the amino acids from your muscles, skin, and even your bones, and transport them to the liver to be converted into glucose.




This constant, cortisol-driven stream of endogenous glucose production is the real, often-overlooked driver of insulin resistance. The common narrative blames dietary carbohydrates, but in reality, **the elevated blood glucose that characterizes insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes is primarily driven by cortisol-induced gluconeogenesis.** 




The body is not just struggling with the sugar coming in; it's drowning in the sugar it is being forced to make from its own tissues.




This creates a vicious cycle. The chronically elevated glucose requires a chronically high insulin response, which over time leads to the cells becoming "deaf" or resistant to insulin's signal. The pancreas has to shout louder and louder (secrete more insulin) to get the same effect, leading to a state of hyperinsulinemia that drives fat storage and further inflammation.




Therefore, T2D should be viewed first and foremost as an endocrine disorder—a disease of hormonal dysregulation—not simply a disease of diet. The problem starts with the chronic stress signal that keeps cortisol elevated, turning your body into a sugar-producing factory that works against itself. Controlling stress and inhibiting cortisol's damaging effects are therefore non-negotiable steps in reversing insulin resistance and restoring metabolic health.
